# Home Inspection Cost Breakdown: What You Should Actually Pay for Pre-Purchase and Specialized Inspections

You're buying a house. Your real estate agent says "get an inspection" like it's a checkbox item. You Google it, find a guy for $350, and three days later he hands you a 40-page report full of terrifying phrases like "evidence of moisture intrusion" and "recommend further evaluation by a licensed specialist."

Now you're wondering: Is this house a money pit? Should you walk away? Or is the inspector just covering his liability?

Home inspections are one of the few services where you're paying someone to find problems. The question is whether they find the RIGHT problems — and whether the cost of additional specialized inspections is worth it for your specific situation.

Here's what inspections actually cost, what they cover, and when to pay for extras.

## The General Home Inspection

### What It Covers

A standard home inspection follows ASHI (American Society of Home Inspectors) or InterNACHI standards and covers:

**Exterior:**
- Roof condition (from ground or ladder, not always walking the roof)
- Siding, trim, flashing
- Grading and drainage around foundation
- Driveways, walkways, patios (visible defects)
- Decks, porches, railings

**Interior:**
- Foundation/basement/crawlspace (visible portions)
- Structural components (visible framing, load-bearing walls)
- Plumbing (fixtures, visible piping, water pressure, drainage)
- Electrical (panels, outlets, GFCI protection, visible wiring)
- HVAC (operation, age, basic function)
- Insulation and ventilation (visible/accessible)
- Windows and doors
- Interior walls, ceilings, floors (visible defects)
- Built-in appliances (dishwasher, oven, range, disposal)
- Garage (door, opener, fire separation)
- Attic (if accessible)

### What It Does NOT Cover

This is where confusion lives. A general inspection is a **visual, non-invasive evaluation.** Inspectors don't:

- Move furniture, stored items, or personal belongings
- Open walls, ceilings, or floors
- Enter areas that are inaccessible or unsafe
- Test for radon, mold, lead, asbestos, or environmental hazards
- Inspect inside sewer lines
- Perform engineering calculations
- Inspect pools, spas, outbuildings (unless specifically agreed)
- Guarantee future performance of any system
- Provide repair cost estimates (in most states)

**The inspection is a snapshot, not a warranty.** It identifies visible defects and red flags that warrant further investigation.

### General Inspection Pricing

| **Home Size** | **Typical Cost** |
|---------------|------------------|
| **Under 1,500 sqft** | $275-$400 |
| **1,500-2,500 sqft** | $350-$500 |
| **2,500-3,500 sqft** | $400-$600 |
| **3,500-5,000 sqft** | $500-$750 |
| **5,000+ sqft** | $700-$1,200+ |

**Factors that increase price:**
- Age of home (older homes take longer)
- Crawlspace access (adds time)
- Outbuildings (detached garage, barn, shed)
- Multi-unit properties
- Rural/remote location (travel surcharge)

### What You Get

A comprehensive written report (30-80+ pages) with:
- Photos of every defect and concern
- Categorization (safety hazard, major defect, minor defect, maintenance item)
- Recommendations for further evaluation where needed
- Summary of major systems (age, condition, expected lifespan)

**Turnaround:** Same day to 48 hours. Most inspectors provide digital reports within 24 hours.

## Specialized Inspections: When Each One Is Worth It

### Radon Testing — $125-$250

**What it is:** Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps from soil into homes. It's the #2 cause of lung cancer (after smoking). Colorless, odorless — you can't detect it without testing.

**How it works:** Inspector places a continuous radon monitor (CRM) in the lowest livable level for 48+ hours. Digital monitors provide hour-by-hour readings.

**When to do it:** **Almost always.** The EPA recommends testing every home. Radon varies block by block — your neighbor's reading doesn't predict yours.

| **Radon Level** | **EPA Recommendation** |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Below 2 pCi/L** | Low risk, no action needed |
| **2-4 pCi/L** | Consider mitigation |
| **Above 4 pCi/L** | Mitigation recommended |
| **Above 8 pCi/L** | Mitigation strongly recommended |

**Mitigation cost (if needed):** $800-$2,500 for a sub-slab depressurization system. This is a common negotiation point — ask the seller to install or credit at closing.

**Worth it?** Yes. $150 now vs. cancer risk later. Not a close call.

### Termite/Wood-Destroying Insect (WDI) Inspection — $75-$200

**What it is:** Licensed pest inspector checks for termites, carpenter ants, powder post beetles, and wood-boring insects plus evidence of wood rot and moisture damage.

**When to do it:** Always. Many lenders (especially FHA/VA) require it.

**What they find:**
- Active infestations
- Previous damage/treatment evidence
- Conditions conducive to infestation (wood-soil contact, moisture)

**Treatment costs (if active infestation):**
| **Treatment** | **Cost** |
|---------------|----------|
| **Spot treatment (localized)** | $200-$600 |
| **Liquid barrier treatment (perimeter)** | $800-$2,500 |
| **Bait system (Sentricon, etc.)** | $1,200-$3,500 initial + $200-$400/year |
| **Fumigation (tent, drywood termites)** | $1,500-$5,000+ |
| **Structural repair (if damage)** | $1,000-$10,000+ |

**Worth it?** Absolutely. Termite damage costs homeowners billions annually. $100 for early detection is a no-brainer.

### Sewer/Drain Camera Inspection — $125-$400

**What it is:** A camera on a flexible cable is fed through the main sewer line from the cleanout to the street/septic connection. Records video of pipe condition.

**What it finds:**
- Root intrusion
- Cracked, collapsed, or offset pipes
- Bellied (sagging) sections
- Blockages
- Pipe material (clay, cast iron, PVC, Orangeburg)
- Connection quality

**When to do it:**
- Homes built before 1970 (likely clay or cast iron pipes)
- Any home with mature trees near the sewer line
- Homes with a history of drain backups
- Homes with Orangeburg (bituminous fiber) pipe (1940s-1970s — this material is essentially cardboard and is always failing)

**Repair costs if problems found:**
| **Repair** | **Cost** |
|------------|----------|
| **Root clearing** | $200-$600 |
| **Spot repair (per excavation)** | $1,500-$4,000 |
| **Pipe lining (trenchless)** | $4,000-$15,000 |
| **Full sewer line replacement** | $5,000-$25,000 |

**Worth it?** For homes 25+ years old: absolutely. A $200 camera inspection can reveal a $15,000 problem before you own it. For new construction or homes with verified PVC lines: skip it.

### Mold Inspection — $300-$700

**What it is:** A qualified mold inspector takes air samples (inside and outside for comparison) and/or surface samples, then sends them to a lab for species identification and spore count.

**When to do it:**
- Visible mold growth
- Musty odors
- History of water damage or flooding
- High humidity/moisture readings found during general inspection
- You or family members have mold sensitivities

**When NOT to do it:**
- No signs of moisture or mold
- Home is dry with good ventilation
- Just because someone suggested it "to be safe"

**Mold remediation costs (if needed):**
| **Area** | **Cost** |
|----------|----------|
| **Small area (under 10 sqft)** | $500-$1,500 |
| **Moderate (10-100 sqft)** | $1,500-$5,000 |
| **Large (100+ sqft, structural)** | $3,000-$15,000+ |
| **Full basement remediation** | $5,000-$30,000 |

**Worth it?** Only if there are indicators. Routine mold testing in a dry, well-maintained home is unnecessary. But if the general inspector noted moisture, staining, or musty odors: get the test.

**Red flag:** Mold inspectors who also do mold remediation have a conflict of interest. The inspection company should NOT be the same company that does the cleanup.

### Structural Engineer Inspection — $300-$800

**What it is:** A licensed Professional Engineer (PE) evaluates structural integrity — foundation, load-bearing walls, beams, floor systems, and settlement patterns.

**When to do it:**
- General inspector flags structural concerns (cracking, bowing, sagging)
- Visible foundation cracks wider than 1/4"
- Uneven floors (more than 1/2" over 20 feet)
- Doors/windows that don't close properly
- Previous foundation repairs
- You're buying an older home (pre-1950) with uncertain history

**What you get:** A written engineering report with:
- Assessment of structural condition
- Identification of defects (cosmetic vs. structural)
- Recommended repairs with priority ranking
- Estimated repair costs (sometimes)
- Stamped by a licensed PE (carries legal weight)

**Worth it?** When structural concerns exist, absolutely. A $500 engineering assessment is nothing compared to a $30,000 surprise foundation repair. And the PE's report carries weight in price negotiations — it's expert opinion, not a home inspector's observation.

### Chimney Inspection — $150-$500

**What it is:** CSIA-certified chimney sweep inspects the chimney structure, flue liner, firebox, damper, cap, and crown.

**Three levels:**
| **Level** | **What's Checked** | **Cost** | **When** |
|-----------|--------------------|----------|----------|
| **Level 1** | Visual check of accessible areas | $100-$250 | Annual maintenance |
| **Level 2** | Level 1 + camera scan of flue interior | $200-$500 | Property transfer (buying/selling) |
| **Level 3** | Level 2 + opening walls/ceilings for hidden issues | $500-$2,000+ | After chimney fire or structural concern |

**For home purchases:** Level 2 is appropriate.

**Common chimney issues found:**
| **Problem** | **Repair Cost** |
|-------------|-----------------|
| **Cracked flue liner** | $1,500-$5,000 (reline) |
| **Damaged crown** | $300-$1,000 |
| **Missing/damaged cap** | $150-$500 |
| **Deteriorated mortar** | $500-$2,500 (tuckpointing) |
| **Creosote buildup** | $200-$400 (cleaning) |
| **Structural lean/separation** | $2,000-$10,000+ |

**Worth it?** Yes, if the home has a working fireplace and you plan to use it. A compromised chimney is a fire/CO risk.

### Well Water Testing — $100-$500

**What it is:** Laboratory analysis of water quality from private wells.

**Basic panel ($100-$200):** Coliform bacteria, nitrates, pH, turbidity
**Comprehensive panel ($200-$500):** Basic + heavy metals (lead, arsenic), VOCs, pesticides, minerals, hardness

**When to do it:** ALWAYS for homes with private wells. Non-negotiable.

**FHA/VA loans require it.** Even for conventional loans, skip this at your own peril. Well water quality can change dramatically, and treatment systems are expensive:

| **Treatment** | **Cost** |
|---------------|----------|
| **UV disinfection (bacteria)** | $500-$2,000 |
| **Arsenic removal** | $1,500-$5,000 |
| **Iron/manganese filtration** | $800-$3,000 |
| **Water softener** | $800-$3,000 |
| **Reverse osmosis (whole-home)** | $1,500-$5,000 |
| **New well (if contaminated/failing)** | $5,000-$15,000+ |

### Septic Inspection — $250-$600

**What it is:** Inspect septic tank condition, measure sludge level, check drain field operation, verify system size vs. home bedrooms.

**When to do it:** ALWAYS for homes with septic systems. Also non-negotiable.

**What's checked:**
- Tank condition and capacity
- Sludge/scum levels (pumping needed?)
- Baffles and inlet/outlet condition
- Distribution box (if present)
- Drain field condition (standing water, odor, surface seeping)
- System age and maintenance history

**Septic repair/replacement costs:**
| **Service** | **Cost** |
|-------------|----------|
| **Routine pumping** | $250-$500 |
| **Baffle repair** | $300-$800 |
| **Distribution box replacement** | $500-$1,500 |
| **Drain field repair** | $2,000-$10,000 |
| **Complete system replacement** | $10,000-$30,000+ |

**Worth it?** A failed septic system is one of the most expensive hidden problems in a home. $400 for an inspection saves potential five-figure headaches.

## How to Choose a Home Inspector

### What Makes a Good Inspector

1. **Credentials:** ASHI-certified or InterNACHI-certified. Both require training, testing, and continuing education.

2. **Experience:** Minimum 200-300 inspections. Ideally 1,000+. Ask how many they've done.

3. **Insurance:** E&O (errors and omissions) insurance protects you if they miss something major. Ask if they carry it.

4. **Sample report:** Request one before hiring. Look for:
   - Clear photos with annotations
   - Organized by system/category
   - Severity ratings (safety, major, minor, maintenance)
   - Specific locations of defects
   - Not just a checklist — narrative descriptions

5. **Time on site:** A thorough inspection of a 2,000 sqft home takes 2.5-4 hours. If someone offers to do it in 90 minutes, they're cutting corners.

6. **Specializations:** Some inspectors hold additional certifications (radon, mold, thermal imaging). Multi-certified inspectors can handle more in one visit.

### What to Avoid

1. **Your agent's "preferred" inspector** — Not always a conflict of interest, but be aware: some agents prefer inspectors who don't "kill deals." An inspector whose livelihood depends on agent referrals may soft-pedal concerns. You want an inspector who works for YOU.

2. **Cheapest option** — A $200 inspection for a $400,000 house is not a deal. It's 30 minutes and a checklist. The good inspectors charge $350-$600 because they spend 3-4 hours being thorough.

3. **No sample report available** — If they can't show you their work product before you hire them, move on.

4. **"Everything looks fine" inspectors** — Every house has defects. If the report is suspiciously clean, the inspector missed things or chose not to report them.

## What to Do With the Report

### Categorizing Findings

**Safety hazards (address immediately):**
- No GFCI protection in wet areas
- Open junction boxes
- Water heater lacking TPR valve/discharge pipe
- Gas leak indicators
- Trip/fall hazards

**Major defects (negotiate or budget for):**
- Roof nearing end of life
- HVAC system aging/failing
- Foundation concerns
- Active water intrusion
- Electrical panel issues (Federal Pacific, Zinsco)

**Minor defects (maintenance items):**
- Caulking gaps
- Loose outlet covers
- Missing dryer vent cap
- Sticking doors/windows
- Minor grading issues

### Negotiation Strategy

**What to ask the seller for:**
1. **Repair credits** (preferred) — Seller credits you money at closing. You control the repair quality.
2. **Price reduction** — Lower the purchase price by estimated repair cost.
3. **Seller-completed repairs** — Least preferred. Sellers often choose the cheapest contractor and minimum fix.

**What's reasonable to negotiate:**
- Safety hazards: Always
- Major defects: Usually
- Minor defects: Rarely (these are expected in any used home)
- Cosmetic issues: Never

**Rule of thumb:** Focus negotiations on items that cost $500+ to repair. A 40-page report with 60 items can look scary, but if 55 of them are $50 fixes, the house is in good shape.

## DIY Inspection: Can You Skip the Professional?

**Short answer: No.** Not for a purchase.

**Longer answer:** If you're knowledgeable about construction, you can do your own pre-offer walkthrough to identify obvious issues. But a professional inspector:
- Has seen thousands of homes and recognizes patterns
- Uses specialized tools (thermal camera, moisture meter, gas detector, outlet tester)
- Provides a legal document for negotiation and due diligence
- Checks areas you might not think to look (attic, crawlspace, electrical panel internals)
- Carries insurance if they miss something

**For non-purchase situations** (annual maintenance checks on your own home), self-inspection is fine. InterNACHI publishes free home maintenance checklists.

## Red Flags & What to Watch For

### Inspector Red Flags

1. **Inspection takes less than 2 hours** for a standard home — they're not being thorough.

2. **Verbal-only report** — Everything should be written and photographed. "I'll tell you what I found" is not a deliverable.

3. **Won't let you attend** — You should be at the inspection (at least for the final walkthrough). Good inspectors welcome questions and explain findings.

4. **Makes repair estimates or referrals** — Most state licensing boards prohibit inspectors from providing repair estimates or recommending specific contractors (conflict of interest). An inspector who says "I know a guy who can fix that" is a red flag.

5. **Doesn't go on the roof, into the crawlspace, or open the electrical panel** — These are standard parts of a home inspection. If conditions are unsafe (ice on roof, flooded crawlspace), they should document why they couldn't access and recommend follow-up.

### Specialized Inspection Red Flags

1. **Mold company that does testing AND remediation** — The company finding mold should not be the one profiting from cleanup. Separate inspectors and remediators.

2. **"Comprehensive" package deals at suspiciously low prices** — "General inspection + radon + mold + sewer scope for $299!" Either they're cutting corners on each, or the add-ons are cursory.

3. **Sewer scope company that "finds" problems requiring expensive repairs AND offers to do the repairs** — Same conflict of interest as mold.

4. **Inspector who can't explain findings in plain language** — If they can't tell you what the problem IS, what it MEANS, and how URGENT it is, the report is useless.
