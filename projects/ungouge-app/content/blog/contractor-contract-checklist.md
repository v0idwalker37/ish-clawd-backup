# What Should a Contractor Contract Include? (Complete Checklist)

*Meta Title: Contractor Contract Checklist: 15 Things That Must Be in Writing (2026)*
*Meta Description: Before signing any contractor agreement, make sure these 15 things are in writing. Missing even one can cost you thousands. Free checklist inside.*

---

A contractor contract isn't just paperwork. It's the only thing standing between you and "I thought you said that was included."

Most homeowner horror stories start with a vague contract. A well-written contract doesn't prevent all problems — but it gives you clear recourse when problems happen. Here's exactly what needs to be in there.

---

## The 15 Things Every Contractor Contract Must Include

### 1. Complete Contact Information for Both Parties

The contract should identify:
- **Contractor:** Full legal business name, business address (not just P.O. box), phone, email, license number, insurance policy number
- **Homeowner:** Your full legal name and the property address where work will be performed

**Why it matters:** If you need to take legal action, you need their full legal name and address. "Bob's Contracting" with only a cell phone number is insufficient.

---

### 2. Detailed Scope of Work

This is the most important section. It should describe, in plain language:
- Exactly what will be done
- What materials will be used (brand, model, grade, quantity)
- What work is **not** included (explicit exclusions matter)
- Who is responsible for permits, inspections, and sign-offs

**Vague is bad:** "Install new flooring throughout main level"

**Good:** "Install 850 sq ft of Bruce Hardwood Collection 3/4" solid oak hardwood flooring in medium brown (Product #DH426) in living room, dining room, and hallway. Includes removal and disposal of existing carpet, installation of 1/4" underlayment, all transition strips and trim. Does not include furniture moving or basement level."

The more specific, the better. If they can't pin down the materials, that's a red flag on its own.

---

### 3. Payment Schedule

**Never pay 100% upfront.** A typical payment schedule:
- **10-30% deposit** to secure materials and schedule
- **Progress payments** tied to completion milestones (not time)
- **10% final payment** held until job completion and walkthrough

Example for a kitchen remodel:
- 20% upon signing (materials purchase)
- 30% when demolition and rough work complete
- 30% when cabinets installed and rough plumbing/electrical done
- 10% upon substantial completion
- 10% upon final walkthrough and punch list completion

**Red flag payment structures:**
- >50% deposit before work begins
- Payment tied to dates rather than milestones
- "Pay as we go" with no written schedule
- Requests for cash or wire transfers only

---

### 4. Start Date and Estimated Completion Date

Include both with realistic buffers:
- "Work will begin on or about [date]"
- "Substantial completion expected by [date], weather permitting"
- "Project considered substantially complete when [specific milestone]"

**Also include:** What happens if they miss the timeline. Some contracts include penalty clauses ($X per day for delays caused by the contractor). These are harder to enforce but signal contractor accountability.

---

### 5. Change Order Process

Changes happen. The question is how they're handled.

The contract should specify:
- All changes to scope, materials, or cost must be in writing (change order form)
- Change orders must be signed by both parties before additional work begins
- Change order price is agreed upon before work starts (not billed after)

**Without this:** A contractor can claim they did "extra work" and demand payment, and you have no documented agreement on what was authorized.

---

### 6. Materials and Substitution Policy

List specific materials with:
- Brand, model, grade, color
- Source (contractor supply vs. homeowner-purchased vs. salvage)
- What happens if specified materials are unavailable (substitution requires homeowner approval)

**Critical:** If the contract says "or equivalent," define what equivalent means. "Equivalent" to a contractor often means cheaper; equivalent to you means same quality.

---

### 7. Subcontractor Disclosure

If any work will be subcontracted:
- List which trades will be subcontracted
- Confirm subcontractors are licensed and insured
- Specify that general contractor remains responsible for all work quality

**Why it matters:** If a subcontractor does bad work, the GC is liable — not the sub directly. Make sure the contract reflects this.

---

### 8. Permit and Inspection Requirements

The contract should state:
- Which permits are required
- Who is responsible for pulling permits (almost always the contractor)
- That work will be completed to code and will pass all required inspections
- Contractor will schedule and attend inspections

**Critical:** Work done without permits can be ordered demolished by your municipality. Required permits vary by project type and jurisdiction — ask your local building department if unsure.

---

### 9. Cleanup and Disposal

Often overlooked until you're left with a dumpster's worth of debris on your lawn:
- Daily cleanup expectations
- Debris removal and disposal (who pays, how, where)
- Damage protection for areas adjacent to the work zone
- Final cleanup standards

---

### 10. Warranty Information

Include explicitly:
- **Labor warranty:** Length of coverage for workmanship defects (industry standard: 1 year minimum)
- **Materials warranty:** Pass-through of manufacturer warranties, plus contractor's installation warranty
- **What voids the warranty:** Understanding this protects both parties

Get warranty terms in writing. A verbal warranty exists only in someone's memory.

---

### 11. Dispute Resolution Process

How disagreements are handled matters. Options from least to most formal:
1. **Direct negotiation** (required first)
2. **Mediation** (neutral third party, non-binding, cheaper)
3. **Arbitration** (neutral third party, binding, faster than court)
4. **Litigation** (court, expensive, slow, last resort)

Many contractor contracts now include mandatory arbitration clauses. Read these carefully — they can limit your right to sue.

---

### 12. Lien Waiver Requirements

A mechanic's lien allows contractors and suppliers to place a claim on your property if they're not paid. Even if you pay your contractor in full, that contractor might not pay their suppliers — leaving you exposed.

The contract should require:
- **Final lien waiver from the contractor** upon receiving final payment
- **Conditional lien waivers from major subcontractors** as work progresses

Ask your attorney or title company for state-specific lien waiver forms.

---

### 13. Insurance Requirements

Explicitly state:
- Contractor maintains general liability insurance of at least $1M per occurrence
- Contractor maintains workers' compensation if they have employees
- Contractor will provide updated certificates if policies renew during the project
- Homeowner will not be liable for worker injuries (covered by workers' comp)

---

### 14. Right to Terminate

Conditions under which either party can terminate the contract:
- **For cause:** Material breach (contractor abandons job, homeowner refuses to pay)
- **For convenience:** Either party can exit with reasonable notice (typically 3-7 days) and settlement of work completed

Include what happens to deposit/progress payments upon termination.

---

### 15. Signature and Date Requirements

**Obvious but essential:**
- Both parties sign and date the contract
- Both parties receive a copy
- Any amendments or addenda are also signed by both parties

**Digital signatures** (DocuSign, etc.) are generally legally valid for contractor agreements. Email confirmations are not a substitute for a signed contract.

---

## Contract Red Flags

Walk away or demand revision if:
- ❌ Contract is less than 2-3 pages (scope too vague to be enforceable)
- ❌ Payment exceeds 30% before work starts
- ❌ Change orders are verbal ("we'll figure it out")
- ❌ Contractor resists putting specifics in writing
- ❌ Materials aren't specified ("standard quality")
- ❌ No timeline or milestones
- ❌ Warranty is verbal only

---

## Contract Checklists by Project Type

For larger projects, additional terms matter:

**Kitchen/Bathroom Remodel:**
- Temporary kitchen/bathroom arrangements during construction?
- Appliance delivery coordination
- When can you use the space?

**Roofing:**
- Ice and water shield requirements
- Ventilation specifications
- What happens if decking damage is found under old shingles?

**HVAC Replacement:**
- EPA certification of technicians (legally required for refrigerant handling)
- Old equipment disposal
- System startup and commissioning

**New Construction:**
- Draw schedule tied to inspections
- Construction loan coordination
- Certificate of occupancy responsibility
