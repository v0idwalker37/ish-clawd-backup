# What is a Fair Contractor Markup in 2026?

*Published: February 19, 2026*

*Meta Description: Understanding contractor markup percentages helps you spot overpricing. Learn what's fair in 2026, industry standards, and when to negotiate.*

If you've ever looked at a contractor's quote and thought "this seems high," you're not alone. The markup — the difference between what materials and labor actually cost versus what you're charged — can feel like a black box.

Let's clear that up.

## The Short Answer

**Fair contractor markup in 2026 ranges from 15% to 40%**, depending on project complexity, overhead, and market conditions.

- **Materials**: 10-20% markup is standard
- **Labor**: 20-40% markup (covers overhead, insurance, licensing)
- **Total project**: 15-35% overall markup is typical for residential work

Anything above 40% deserves scrutiny. Anything below 10% might signal corner-cutting.

## Why Contractors Mark Up (And Why It's Not Evil)

Markup isn't profit. Most of it goes to:

- **Overhead**: Office, trucks, tools, insurance, licensing
- **Benefits**: Health insurance, workers' comp for employees
- **Risk**: Warranties, callbacks, project delays
- **Actual profit**: Usually 8-15% after all costs

A contractor charging 30% markup might net 10% profit after overhead. That's not gouging — that's running a business.

## What's Fair vs. What's Gouging

### Fair Markup Examples

**Kitchen remodel in Denver, CO:**
- Materials: $12,000 + 15% markup = $13,800
- Labor: 200 hours × $65/hr = $13,000 + 25% markup = $16,250
- **Total quoted: $30,050** (20% overall markup)

**Bathroom renovation in Phoenix, AZ:**
- Materials: $8,000 + 18% markup = $9,440
- Labor: 120 hours × $55/hr = $6,600 + 30% markup = $8,580
- **Total quoted: $18,020** (25% overall markup)

These are within normal range.

### Red Flag Markup Examples

**Deck build in Austin, TX:**
- Materials: $4,000 + **60% markup** = $6,400 (⚠️ Too high)
- Labor: 80 hours × $50/hr = $4,000 + **55% markup** = $6,200 (⚠️ Too high)
- **Total quoted: $12,600** (57% overall markup)

This contractor is either inexperienced (high waste/inefficiency) or overcharging.

## How to Spot Excessive Markup

### 1. Round Numbers Everywhere

If every line item ends in $500 or $1,000, that's a sign of ballpark pricing with padding built in. Fair quotes break down to actual unit costs.

### 2. Vague Line Items

- ❌ "Labor: $8,500"
- ❌ "Materials: $12,000"
- ✅ "Tile installation: 120 sq ft × $8.50/sq ft = $1,020"

The more detail, the harder it is to hide excessive markup.

### 3. "Contingency" Line Items

A 5-10% contingency for unforeseen issues is reasonable. Anything over 15% is padding.

### 4. Markup on Subcontractors

Some contractors mark up subcontractor work by 20-30% just for "project management." That's double-dipping if they're already charging you a project management fee.

## Regional Differences Matter

Markup varies by market:

| Region | Typical Markup |
|--------|----------------|
| High-cost cities (SF, NYC, Boston) | 25-40% |
| Mid-size metros (Denver, Austin, Phoenix) | 20-30% |
| Rural/small towns | 15-25% |

Higher cost-of-living = higher overhead = higher markup. A 35% markup in San Francisco might be fair; the same markup in rural Iowa is not.

## When to Negotiate

You have leverage when:

1. **Markup exceeds 40%** on materials or 50% on labor
2. **Project total is high** ($50k+) — even 5% off is meaningful
3. **You're paying cash upfront** — that's valuable to contractors
4. **It's off-season** (winter for exteriors, summer for HVAC)

### How to Negotiate Without Being That Person

**Don't say:** "Your prices are too high."

**Do say:** "I've gotten quotes in the $X range for similar work. Can you help me understand the difference?"

Give them a chance to justify. Sometimes there's a good reason (better materials, longer warranty, etc.). Sometimes they'll adjust.
