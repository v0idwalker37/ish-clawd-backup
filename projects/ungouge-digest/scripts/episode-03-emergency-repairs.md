### ACT 2: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF EMERGENCY PRICING [3:30-5:30]

**[VISUAL: Split screen showing damaged home vs. pristine home]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Why do homeowners pay these prices? It's not stupidity. It's psychology.

**[VISUAL: Animated brain with labeled sections lighting up]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**First: Stress impairs judgment.** Studies on decision-making under stress show that people in crisis situations focus on immediate threat reduction, not cost optimization. Your roof is leaking into your living room. You're not thinking about whether eighty dollars per hour for labor is fair. You're thinking "make it stop."

**[VISUAL: Stress hormone graph spiking, with decision-making quality graph dropping]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Second: Scarcity bias.** When contractors say "I can start tomorrow, but I'm booking up fast," that triggers scarcity bias. We overvalue immediate availability and undervalue price considerations.

**[VISUAL: Animation of a contractor calendar filling up with red "BOOKED" stamps]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Third: Authority bias.** After a disaster, contractors show up with expertise you desperately need. They're assessing damage you don't fully understand. That information asymmetry is even more extreme than normal, and we tend to defer to perceived authority.

**[VISUAL: Contractor pointing at roof damage with homeowner nodding uncertainly]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**And fourth: The insurance buffer.** If insurance is covering it, the money doesn't feel "real" in the same way. Studies show people are willing to accept thirty to forty percent higher prices when someone else is ostensibly paying.

**[VISUAL: Psychological price thresholds comparison: Out-of-pocket vs. Insurance-covered]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Contractors know all of this, and the smart ones — ethical or not — use it.

### ACT 3: WHEN TO WAIT, WHEN TO ACT [5:30-7:30]

**[VISUAL: Decision tree graphic]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
So here's the critical question: when do you actually need to pay emergency prices, and when can you wait?

**[VISUAL: Category 1: TRUE EMERGENCIES - red alert style]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**True Emergencies — Pay the Premium:**  
Active water intrusion that's causing ongoing damage. Yes, pay for emergency tarping, even at inflated prices. The alternative is tens of thousands in additional water damage.

**[VISUAL: Example of water damage spreading through a ceiling]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Electrical hazards — downed lines, exposed wiring in wet areas. Pay for immediate electrical safety work. This is life-safety, not just property.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Gas leaks. Obviously.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Structural instability that poses collapse risk. If an adjuster or inspector says "this is unsafe to occupy," you need immediate shoring or temporary supports.

**[VISUAL: Category 2: URGENT BUT NOT EMERGENCY - yellow caution style]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Urgent But Not Emergency — You Have Negotiating Power:**  
Tarps are up, but you need permanent roof repair. The immediate crisis is handled. You can get multiple quotes. Take a week. Prices will start to normalize after the initial rush.

**[VISUAL: Timeline showing price normalization curve after disasters]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Damaged siding or windows that are temporarily boarded up. Not ideal, but you can wait for better pricing.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Fence damage, cosmetic exterior damage. Insurance might cover it, but there's no rush.

**[VISUAL: Category 3: NON-URGENT - green "wait" style]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Non-Urgent — Definitely Wait:**  
Interior cosmetic repairs. Drywall, paint, flooring in rooms that aren't actively being damaged — wait until the market stabilizes, typically thirty to sixty days post-disaster.

**[VISUAL: Price comparison: Week 1 vs. Week 8 post-disaster for the same interior work]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
The same interior work that costs eighteen thousand two weeks after a hurricane might cost eleven thousand if you wait two months. If you have somewhere else to stay, that patience saves you thousands.

### ACT 4: HOW TO PROTECT YOURSELF [7:30-9:15]

**[VISUAL: Shield icon with checklist items appearing]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Here's how to protect yourself when emergency repairs are actually necessary.

**[VISUAL: Tip #1 animation]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**One: Document everything before the contractor arrives.** Photos, videos, detailed notes. This protects you if there are disputes with insurance or if the contractor tries to claim additional damage.

**[VISUAL: Phone camera footage of damage documentation]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Two: Get it in writing, even for emergency work.** A verbal "I'll tarp your roof for five hundred" can become "the tarp and emergency assessment was two thousand" when the bill arrives. Text message confirmation counts.

**[VISUAL: Example text exchange confirming scope and price]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Three: Separate emergency work from permanent repairs.** Don't sign a contract for a full roof replacement when you're in crisis mode. Pay for the tarp. Then, when you're thinking clearly, get quotes for the permanent fix.

**[VISUAL: Two separate contracts highlighted]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Four: Know your state's price-gouging laws.** Some states have price-gouging protections that kick in after declared emergencies. In Texas, for example, prices can't exceed what the seller charged for the same goods or services in the preceding thirty days by more than ten percent. In Florida, it's even stricter.

**[VISUAL: Map of US showing states with price-gouging laws highlighted]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Most contractors either don't know this or are counting on you not knowing it. If a contractor who normally charges three thousand for a tarp service is suddenly charging nine thousand, that may be illegal.

**[VISUAL: State price-gouging enforcement hotline numbers appearing]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Five: Your insurance company is your leverage.** Adjusters see inflated post-disaster quotes constantly. If a quote seems high, send it to your adjuster before agreeing. They'll often tell you directly if it's inflated and suggest alternatives.

**[VISUAL: Email exchange with insurance adjuster]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Six: Use public data even in emergencies.** It takes five minutes to check BLS wage data for your area. If a contractor is billing electrician time at two hundred fifty dollars an hour in a market where electricians make forty dollars an hour, you have ammunition to negotiate.

**[VISUAL: Side-by-side of BLS data and contractor quote, highlighting discrepancy]**
