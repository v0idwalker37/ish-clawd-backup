**[VISUAL: Animated flowchart showing the complexity of a kitchen remodel]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Kitchen remodels are the perfect storm for overcharging, and it comes down to three things: complexity, emotion, and opacity.

**[VISUAL: Split-screen showing multiple trades: plumber, electrician, cabinet installer, tile setter, etc.]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
First, complexity. A kitchen remodel typically involves six to eight different trades. Demolition, plumbing, electrical, drywall, cabinet installation, countertops, tiling, painting. Most general contractors subcontract all of this out.

**[VISUAL: Animated diagram showing money flow: Homeowner → GC → Subs, with GC taking a cut at each stage]**

**[VOICEOVER]**

**[VISUAL: Pinterest/Instagram feed scrolling past dream kitchens]**

**[VOICEOVER]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Third, opacity. Most kitchen remodel quotes are given as a lump sum. No detailed breakdown. Just "full kitchen remodel: forty-two thousand dollars." You have no idea what you're actually paying for.

### ACT 3: RED FLAGS IN KITCHEN QUOTES [5:30-7:15]

**[VISUAL: Red flag animation, transition to list format]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
So how do you spot an inflated kitchen quote? Here are five red flags.

**[VISUAL: Red flag #1 appears]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Red Flag One: No itemization.** If the quote just says "kitchen remodel" with a total, that's a problem. You should see separate line items for cabinets, countertops, appliances, each trade's labor, etc.

**[VISUAL: Example of good vs. bad quote formats side by side]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Red Flag Two: Appliances marked up more than fifteen percent.** Appliance costs are public information. If a quote charges you seven thousand for appliances you can find for five thousand at Home Depot, they're padding.

**[VISUAL: Red flag #2, screenshot of appliance prices on retail sites]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Red Flag Three: Vague cabinet descriptions.** "Kitchen cabinets: eighteen thousand dollars" tells you nothing. Stock, semi-custom, or full custom? What brand? Plywood or particle board boxes? Soft-close hinges? Specifics matter.

**[VISUAL: Red flag #3, examples of cabinet quality differences]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Red Flag Four: Labor billed as "project management" at inflated rates.** Some quotes include a separate twenty to thirty percent "project management fee" *on top of* profit margin. That's double-dipping.

**[VISUAL: Red flag #4, calculator showing the double-markup]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Red Flag Five: Pressure to decide quickly.** "This price is only good for forty-eight hours." Real contractors don't do this. It's a sales tactic to prevent you from getting other quotes or doing research.

**[VISUAL: Red flag #5, countdown timer animation with big X through it]**

### ACT 4: HOW TO NEGOTIATE WITH DATA [7:15-8:45]

**[VISUAL: Transition to negotiation scenario animation]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Here's the reality: most contractors will negotiate if you come armed with data. But you have to do it right.

**[VISUAL: Step-by-step process graphics]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Step one:** Get at least three quotes. This gives you a range and leverage.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Step two:** Ask for itemized breakdowns. If they won't provide one, that's a red flag by itself.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Step three:** Run the numbers through a tool like Ungouge.ai or manually price out materials at retail. This gives you a baseline.

**[VISUAL: Screen recording of pricing out materials on Home Depot website]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Step four:** If the quote is significantly higher than the data suggests, bring it up directly. Say something like: "I priced out these cabinets at seventy-five hundred retail. You're charging fourteen thousand. Can you explain the difference?"

**[VISUAL: Mock conversation bubbles showing professional negotiation dialogue]**

**[VOICEOVER]**  
Good contractors will explain their value-add. Maybe they're including design services, or they have a particularly skilled installer, or they offer a better warranty. That's fair.

**[VOICEOVER]**  
**Step five:** If they can't justify it, negotiate. Most contractors have about ten to fifteen percent wiggle room built into their quotes. If they immediately drop twenty to thirty percent, you know the original quote was inflated.

**[VISUAL: Price dropping animation, showing negotiation progression]**
